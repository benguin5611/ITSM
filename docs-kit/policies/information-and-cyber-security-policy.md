# Information & Cyber Security Policy

> Part of the [docs-kit](../README.md#before-you-rely-on-anything-here) — a Department-of-One starting point, not a certified or audit-ready control out of the box. Read that disclaimer and this artefact's own "Adapt this to your context" section before relying on it.

The single top-level policy everything else in this kit hangs off. It is deliberately generic and strategic — a policy this short-lived would mean re-signing executive sponsorship every time a control changes, which defeats the point of having a Policy layer at all. The Standards and Procedures underneath it are where the detail — and the churn — lives.

## Introduction

### Context

This policy operates within the organisation's operational landscape, industry-specific requirements, and the evolving cyber threat landscape. It acknowledges the critical role of information assets, the potential impact of security breaches, and the need for a proactive, comprehensive security approach.

### Purpose

To establish a framework for safeguarding the organisation's information assets and ensuring the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of sensitive data — protecting against unauthorised access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction of information, while maintaining compliance with applicable laws and regulations.

### Scope

This policy extends to all organisational units, employees, contractors, systems, and processes — including any wholly owned subsidiaries — that handle or have access to the organisation's information assets. It covers both digital and physical assets (networks, servers, applications, databases, physical documents, intellectual property), applies at every location (on-premises or remote), and includes external parties with access to those assets.

### Objectives

1. Ensure the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information assets through appropriate security controls, risk management practices, and incident response mechanisms.
2. Establish a clear governance structure for information and cyber security, including roles, responsibilities, and accountability.
3. Foster a security-aware culture through training, awareness programs, and regular communication.
4. Comply with applicable laws, regulations, contractual obligations, and industry best practices related to information security and data protection.
5. Continuously monitor and assess the organisation's security posture, including regular risk assessments, vulnerability management, and security audits.
6. Implement incident response plans to detect, respond to, and recover from security incidents promptly and effectively, minimising impact.
7. Foster a culture of continuous improvement by regularly reviewing and updating security policies, procedures, and controls as threats and technology evolve.
8. Build trust with customers, partners, and stakeholders by protecting their privacy and maintaining compliance with relevant privacy regulations.

## Policy implementation

### Responsible parties

Adapt the role list to the size of the organisation — a department of one still needs to know which hat is on for which decision, even if one person wears all of them.

| Role | Responsibilities |
| --- | --- |
| **CTO / most senior technical sponsor** | Overall responsibility for implementing and maintaining this policy; oversees security controls and technologies; monitors and evaluates the effectiveness of security measures. |
| **CEO / most senior executive sponsor** | Executive support and leadership; ensures sufficient resources are allocated; promotes a security-aware culture; reviews and approves security strategies; is briefed on significant incidents. |
| **CFO / finance lead** | Allocates budget for security initiatives; reviews the financial aspects of security investment; ensures compliance with financial regulations touching data protection and privacy. |
| **Information Security Manager (or the person doing that job part-time)** | Implements and enforces this policy; develops and maintains procedures and guidelines; conducts risk assessments; oversees incident response; monitors and reports on security metrics. |
| **Engineers, designers, delivery leads (if any)** | Integrate security controls into build and delivery processes; address vulnerabilities; participate in incident response; keep current with emerging threats. |
| **Line managers** | Ensure their team's access, training, and incident-reporting obligations under this policy are actually met — see "Requirements applicable to line managers" below. |

### Communication and distribution

1. **Distribution and accessibility** — publish this policy somewhere centralised and easily found (intranet, document system, employee portal); tell people where it lives.
2. **Training and awareness** — run onboarding and periodic refresher sessions; make them practical, not just a read-through.
3. **Acknowledgement and acceptance** — have people acknowledge they've read and understood it, so accountability is traceable.
4. **Communication channels** — reinforce updates through more than one channel (newsletters, team meetings, notice boards).
5. **Ongoing support** — name a point of contact for questions.
6. **Onboarding integration** — fold this policy into new-starter onboarding rather than treating it as a separate, optional read.
7. **Periodic review** — review on a defined cadence; solicit feedback and fold it into updates.

### Policy change requests

1. **Identify the need** — articulate the rationale (new risk, regulatory change, technology change, feedback).
2. **Document the proposed change** — specific sections, what changes, and why.
3. **Impact assessment** — effect on existing processes, controls, resources, and compliance obligations; weigh both positive and negative implications.
4. **Consultation** — loop in security, legal, HR, and risk personnel as relevant.
5. **Review and approval** — a designated approver (e.g. the CTO or most senior sponsor) reviews against organisational goals, compliance requirements, and risk posture, then approves, rejects, or requests changes.
6. **Communication and documentation** — publish the change; update or addend the policy document.
7. **Training** — where the change is material, run a short awareness session.
8. **Implementation and monitoring** — enforce, monitor compliance, and address deviations through corrective action.

### Policy exception process

1. **Identify the need for an exception** — a clearly defined circumstance, supported by a valid reason (business requirement, technical limitation, legal/regulatory obligation).
2. **Document the request** — which policy or control, why, and any supporting evidence.
3. **Evaluate and assess risk** — potential impact on security, compliance, and risk posture; severity, mitigating controls, and alternatives.
4. **Review and approve** — a named approver or committee assesses justification, feasibility, and acceptability.
5. **Document and track** — the justification, approval, and any conditions or limitations, in a central, auditable record.
6. **Communicate** — tell everyone affected the scope, limitations, and duration of the exception.
7. **Monitor and review** — periodically reassess; modify, extend, or revoke as circumstances change.
8. **Report** — give visibility of exceptions granted (and their risk) to senior management on a regular cadence.

## Policy requirements

### Requirements applicable to all staff

1. **Awareness and training** — participate in security and privacy awareness training.
2. **Confidentiality** — maintain confidentiality of sensitive information; never share it with unauthorised parties unless legally required.
3. **Access control** — use access strictly in line with authorised responsibilities; never share credentials; report suspicious activity promptly.
4. **Incident reporting** — report actual or suspected incidents promptly and with enough detail (time, location, people involved, likely impact) to act on.
5. **Compliance with policies and standards** — comply with this policy and the Standards, Processes, and Procedures beneath it.
6. **Physical security** — secure workstations, lock away sensitive documents, restrict access to designated areas.
7. **Safe use of technology** — use organisation-provided technology securely and responsibly; don't install unauthorised software or visit clearly risky sites.
8. **Reporting security concerns** — raise concerns or non-compliance with a supervisor, IT, or the designated security contact; participate honestly in any investigation or audit.

### Requirements applicable to line managers

1. **Leadership and accountability** — model the behaviour; be accountable for your team's compliance culture.
2. **Employee training and awareness** — ensure your team actually completes required training.
3. **Access control and privilege management** — ensure access is granted on role and need; review privileges regularly; revoke or modify promptly on role change or departure.
4. **Incident response and reporting** — know the incident response procedure; report promptly; make sure your team does too.
5. **Compliance monitoring and enforcement** — check in on your team's adherence; don't assume it.
6. **Risk management** — help identify and assess risk in your area; work with the risk function on mitigation.
7. **Communication and education** — pass on policy updates; keep the door open for questions.
8. **Vendor and third-party management** — ensure vendors and contractors with access to information assets meet the same security bar; review their practices before engaging them.

## Principles

Each of these is the seed of a Standard or Procedure in this kit or its companion — see the cross-reference where one exists.

**Acceptable internet use** — define acceptable use of the internet and organisational resources in line with business objectives and this policy; prohibit access to illegal, offensive, or reputationally damaging content, and unlicensed copying or distribution; require professional conduct on organisational systems; prohibit auto-forwarding or external messaging that could leak sensitive information; enforce technical controls (filtering, monitoring) proportionately; allow minimal, reasonable personal use that doesn't introduce risk or hurt productivity.

**Risk management** — run regular, structured risk assessments; apply a consistent methodology reflecting the organisation's risk appetite; document risks with likelihood/impact ratings in a central register; assign owners, timelines, and treatment strategies (mitigate, transfer, avoid, accept); monitor effectiveness and update as the landscape changes.

**Incident response** — maintain a plan for detecting, prioritising, responding to, and recovering from incidents; define roles with named contacts, reviewed at least annually; define escalation and reporting protocols (including to relevant external bodies where applicable); use a structured priority matrix so response effort matches severity; test the plan at least annually; run a post-incident review for the higher-severity incidents. See the [Incident Response Plan](../processes-procedures/incident-response-plan.md).

**Information security management system (ISMS)** — maintain a formal ISMS plan defining scope, principles, roles, and governance; define the organisational structure (governance body, information security roles, reporting lines); align to a recognised framework (ISO/IEC 27001, SOC 2, or similar) and applicable legislation; keep documentation current with version control; review regularly.

**Internal audit and continuous improvement** — run formal internal audits at least annually; assign qualified, independent auditors; document findings and classify them; track corrective actions to closure; feed audit outcomes, risk assessments, and incidents into a continuous-improvement loop.

**Business continuity and disaster recovery** — maintain a contingency planning framework; define recovery objectives per critical system and process; test at least annually; align with a recognised continuity standard.

**Infrastructure resilience** — maintain capacity and backup strategy proportionate to projected growth; monitor and forecast usage; define backup requirements, schedules, and retention; test restoration at least annually.

**Cloud service provider exits** — have a plan for retrieving all organisational data from a cloud provider on exit; understand data portability and SLA exit terms; identify legal/compliance requirements for the transfer.

**Identity access management** — implement centralised, role-based access management with least-privilege and need-to-know defaults; enforce strong authentication (MFA) for privileged and, where possible, all access; review access periodically. See the [Identity and Access Management Standard](../standards/identity-and-access-management-standard.md) and [Access Management & Review procedure](../processes-procedures/access-management-and-review.md).

**Hardware asset management** — maintain formal procedures for acquisition, labelling, tracking, and retirement of hardware; keep a centralised, audited inventory; secure devices against loss or theft; wipe and decommission securely. See the [Company Asset Register](../registers/company-asset-register.md).

**Information asset classification, handling, and control** — classify information by sensitivity and criticality; apply controls proportionate to classification; version-control and secure ISMS documentation; define and enforce retention periods.

**Third-party vendor assurance** — assess the security posture of vendors before onboarding, proportionate to criticality; maintain a central vendor register; reassess periodically. See [Software Onboarding & Vendor Due Diligence](../processes-procedures/software-onboarding-and-vendor-due-diligence.md).

**Information security in project management** — build security requirements into project planning; assess risk at initiation and key stages; validate deliverables against security policy before production.

**Data privacy and record protection** — handle personal and sensitive data lawfully, fairly, and transparently; obtain informed consent where required; apply encryption, access controls, and data minimisation.

**Network and cloud security** — layer network controls (firewalls, intrusion detection, access restriction); keep configuration current and changes documented and reversible; enforce encryption in transit; monitor for anomalies.

**Secure application development and testing** — build security into the development lifecycle from design to deployment where the organisation develops software; keep coding guidance and training current; test before every material release.

**Endpoint management and protection** — deploy supported endpoint protection everywhere; enable host-based firewalls by default; keep signatures and logs current; treat malware detection as a security event.

**Physical security** — control access to sensitive areas and equipment; log and restrict visitor access; review physical access rights periodically.

**Vulnerability management** — run a risk-based vulnerability program; scan and test regularly; track remediation to a defined target, with formally accepted residual risk where a target can't be met.

**Information security and data privacy training and awareness** — train regularly on emerging threats, phishing, social engineering, and data handling; measure effectiveness and update at least annually.

**Corrective action management** — maintain a formal process for identifying, investigating, and closing nonconformities and improvement opportunities.

**Change management** — establish a formal process for planning, approving, and implementing changes; assess security impact; test before deployment; document rationale, risk, and rollback plans.

**After-hours on-call** — maintain a rotating roster with clear escalation paths; keep on-call personnel fit for duty and contactable; refine based on outcomes.

**Logging and threat detection** — maintain sufficient logging to support monitoring, forensics, and compliance; protect logs from tampering; enable real-time alerting; conduct proactive threat hunting.

**Web hosting and domain management** — choose hosting for availability and security; register domains through reputable registrars with secured account details; renew on time; apply standard web security controls (TLS, DNS hardening, access restriction).

## Policy compliance and enforcement

### Examples of non-compliance

**Misconduct** — minor policy violations (e.g. unauthorised personal use of resources), insubordination, non-compliance with specific procedures, minor conflicts of interest.

**Serious misconduct** — theft or fraud, workplace violence or harassment, gross negligence or recklessness, discrimination, breach of confidentiality (including unauthorised disclosure of sensitive data).

### Consequences

Misconduct is typically addressed through disciplinary measures proportionate to severity — verbal or written warnings, counselling, or a performance improvement plan. Serious misconduct may warrant immediate and decisive action, including termination and potential legal consequences.

## Policy monitoring and evaluation

**Internal audit** — independent personnel assess implementation and compliance against an audit plan; identify gaps and recommend improvement.

**External audit** — independent assessors provide an objective view of compliance with standards, regulations, or industry practice.

**Manual audits of controls** — targeted assessment of individual controls by the people responsible for them, through interviews, documentation review, and testing.

**Key considerations** — run monitoring against a defined schedule; document and share results with stakeholders; address gaps through corrective action and follow-up; adapt the process as the environment and threats change; make sure auditors have the expertise to do the job.

### Review

- **Feedback mechanisms** — a designated contact for feedback; multiple channels; periodic surveys or focus groups; an open, proactive culture around raising concerns.
- **Periodic review** — a defined cadence (commonly annual to biennial); a review group spanning security, HR, technology, legal, compliance, and risk as relevant; assess alignment with emerging threats, regulatory change, and lessons learned; solicit stakeholder feedback; benchmark against industry practice.
- **Revisions and updates** — document the rationale for every change; communicate it; ensure stakeholders can access the updated policy.
- **Training and awareness** — run refresher sessions on material changes.

## Conclusion

This policy sets out how the organisation protects its critical systems, data, and infrastructure — the controls, responsibilities, and processes needed to maintain a strong security posture and meet regulatory and operational obligations. Security is not static; neither is this policy. Regular review, feedback, and update cycles keep it aligned with the risk environment it's meant to manage.

## Adapt this to your context

- **Size**: the responsible-parties table assumes named roles (CTO, CFO, ISM, etc.) exist as separate people. A solo operator or small team collapses several of these into one person — keep the *responsibilities* distinct even where the *person* isn't, so it's clear which hat is on for a given decision.
- **Industry**: some sectors (financial services, healthcare, government, critical infrastructure) layer sector-specific mandatory obligations on top of a generic policy like this one — a dedicated regulator, a mandatory breach-notification regime, or personnel security-clearance requirements. Check what your sector adds before treating this as complete.
- **Government vs non-government**: government entities are often required to align to a specific mandated framework (a national government's own cyber security framework or equivalent) rather than choosing one freely; private organisations generally have more latitude in which framework(s) they align to.
- **Compliance program**: if you're pursuing a specific certification, the ISMS and internal-audit sections should explicitly name the framework you're certifying against and its currently-required elements — check the current edition of whichever one applies, since certification frameworks get revised periodically.
- **Jurisdiction**: the privacy and data-protection principles here are deliberately generic; the specific law that applies (its consent, notification, and retention requirements) depends entirely on where you and your customers are.

**Frameworks referenced** (by family — check the current edition of whichever applies to you): ISO/IEC 27001, SOC 2 (Trust Services Criteria), and the privacy/data-protection legislation applicable to your jurisdiction.
